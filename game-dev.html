the problem

Creating a mobile game with a Childlike-Wonder theme. We wanted to exhibit the freedom of carefreely running with fantasy abilities; while making sure each obstacle isn't tied to one form and ability.

how it's built

A form-state machine in Godot 4 with shared collision and per-form ability nodes, so adding a fourth form is a resource file rather than a refactor. Obstacles are hand drawn to not allow one-form-one-solution gameplay. The game is a mix between "fast-paced QTE" feel and puzzle solving. Some obstacles offer different pathway if the player is willing to look. Or try

what I learned

Input buffering isn't polish, it's the whole game. Responsive instant transformation allows players to express their skills in ways we never thought of during testing.


There's also the importance of difficulty scaling. Making a more complex course doesn't guarantee difficulty, neither does difficulty immediately translates to fun.

the problem

Two phones, no server, and a duel decided by who moves first. The real problem isn't UI, it's fairness under network latency — if each phone decides "I fired first" on its own, whoever has the faster network wins, not whoever has the faster reflexes. No backend to lean on, either.

how it's built

The fix is a host-as-referee model — one phone is the single source of truth for timing, the other renders what it decides, so there's only one clock that matters. It runs on raw Network + Bonjour rather than MultipeerConnectivity, trading a hand-rolled wire protocol for tight control over a strict two-peer pairing. The draw reads through CoreMotion; the trigger binds to the volume button, not a touch target, since reaching for the screen breaks the gun illusion.

what I learned

Fairness turned out to be a design problem before a networking one — the fix wasn't optimizing latency, it was picking one authority and no longer trying to reconcile two independent judges.


Mixing UI frameworks is fine when the seam matches a real difference in what each is good at — SwiftUI for menus, SpriteKit for the duel, not an inconsistency.

the problem

The brief was one question: how do you get an ecosystem to feel organic without hand-scripting what each creature does every frame? It needed several genuinely different species sharing enough machinery that adding a sixth would alter the environment just enough.

how it's built

Every cell shares one base class carrying separation, wander and containment; each species overrides exactly one method, _steering(), and inherits everything else, that's the whole extensibility story. The single shared lever is a static temperature float every species answers to differently: one moves faster in the cold, another dies past a threshold, a third just swells. A Godot quirk sat underneath it all: deleted cells linger until the frame ends, so two predators could both claim the same prey.

what I learned

A shared base class earns its keep when subclasses only ever add, never subtract — every species needed separation, wander and containment, none needed to turn any of them off.


One well-chosen shared variable can carry more design depth than a dozen bespoke systems — the temperature dial is just a float, but because each species interprets it differently, it produces cascading, ecosystem-wide consequences a much bigger simulation would usually need to fake.

About

I first tinkered with Godot a long time ago, when a friend invited me to a game jam. At that point, I didn't even know what a "game jam" or "godot" is, but I arrived at a YouTube tutorial anyway. We ended up never submitting due to our very limited expertise in game development. But I got my first taste in making a game, and I've been thinking about it since.

Though my journey took me to web and mobile development, just recently, I got an opportunity to create something with a team of people who shared the same passion. Getting to finally deliver something that I made, in the crowd of a cafe, to someone's hand was nothing short of satisfying.

This time, game development got me by the throat. I could not stop. From Pathoria, to Fira's Pursuit, to Cowboy Shooters - Bang Bang, I just can't satisfy the urge to make more.

I don't have studio credits or formal training. What I do have is the thirst to create, and I'm told that's rarer than it should be.
